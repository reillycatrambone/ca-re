## Authority replaces the ordinary owner signature

In an ordinary sale, the living owner or an authorized representative executes the transaction. Special transfers require a different source of authority: death and succession law, a trust, a court order, a foreclosure power, or another statute. The salesperson's first task is to identify who has authority to act and what conditions limit that authority.

A relative who has keys is not necessarily entitled to list a decedent's house. A beneficiary who expects to inherit is not necessarily the current title holder or authorized estate representative. A lender that holds a deed of trust has security rights, but not unlimited permission to sell outside the applicable foreclosure process.

Distinguish ownership claims from power to administer. The person entitled to receive value from an estate may not be the person authorized to sign the deed during administration. Title and escrow providers commonly require evidence of capacity, appointment, powers, and any relevant orders.

## Death does not produce one universal process

A person who dies with a valid will is **testate**; a person whose property is not disposed of by a valid will may be **intestate** as to that property. A will directs disposition but does not itself guarantee that probate can be avoided. A person can also die partly testate and partly intestate if the will does not dispose of all probate property.

Probate administration addresses assets, debts, claims, taxes, and distribution under court authority. An **executor** is nominated in the will and serves after the required appointment. An **administrator** is appointed when an executor does not serve, commonly where there is no will. Both are personal representatives and owe duties to the estate and interested persons.

**Intestate succession** determines heirs under statute. It is not a rule that the closest person who volunteered to maintain the house owns it. Marital property characterization and family relationships can affect shares. If no entitled private successor exists under the governing law, **escheat** may result in property passing to the state.

### A will, an appointment, and a deed do different work

Suppose a will leaves a house to a niece and nominates a nephew as executor. The niece is the intended beneficiary of that disposition; the nephew is the proposed administrator of the estate in the executor role. Neither fact alone shows that either person may immediately sign a listing or deed without the required authority. Court appointment and the scope of powers must be established where probate administration is necessary.

The estate may need to pay debts or expenses before distributing property. A specific gift in a will does not guarantee the beneficiary will receive the asset unaffected by valid claims and administration requirements. Conversely, the personal representative's control during administration does not make the house the representative's personal property.

For a transaction file, useful evidence can include the death certificate, relevant title documents, court letters, orders, and other authority documents appropriate to the proceeding. The exact requirements belong to the governing law and title review. A salesperson should identify the correct capacity and obtain verification, not choose a signer based on family seniority, possession of keys, or who first called the office.

## Nonprobate transfers

Some property passes outside ordinary probate. Valid joint-tenancy survivorship can transfer the deceased joint tenant's interest to surviving joint tenants. Community property with right of survivorship can provide a similar specified transfer mechanism. Appropriate documents are still needed to establish the death and update the record; survivorship does not mean the public title record updates itself.

A properly funded trust allows the trustee to administer property under the trust's terms. A successor trustee's sale is not automatically the same as a court-confirmed probate sale. Review trust authority and any limits. A person named as a beneficiary is not necessarily empowered to sign for the trust.

A valid transfer-on-death deed can transfer qualifying property under its statutory requirements. It is not equivalent to simply writing a beneficiary's name on an ordinary deed and leaving it unrecorded in a drawer. Execution, witnessing, recording, revocation, and beneficiary rules require compliance with the current statute.

California also provides simplified procedures for qualifying estates and property. For deaths on or after April 1, 2025, the primary-residence petition procedure under Probate Code sections 13151-13154 uses a $750,000 limit, subject to its other conditions. This is a simplified court process, not an exemption for every estate asset. Other procedures have different limits, and periodic adjustments are scheduled. Eligibility depends on property type, value, date of death, and the particular procedure; an old single "small estate limit" does not control every transfer.

### Compare death, sale, and termination of a life estate

A life tenant's death ends that life estate according to its terms. The succeeding interest may belong to a remainderman or revert to the grantor or successors, depending on how it was created. The life tenant's will does not ordinarily convey a life estate beyond the measuring life. This differs from a fee owner's death, which leaves an inheritable ownership interest to be administered or transferred.

Trust property presents another distinction. The successor trustee may have authority to sell under the trust even though no personal representative has been appointed for that property. But the trust must actually hold the relevant interest; merely signing a trust document without transferring the house into it does not establish that the house is administered as trust property.

Survivorship requires verification as well. A deed originally creating joint tenancy may have been affected by a later conveyance or severance. The correct answer follows the interest existing at death, not an assumption based on how the owners first purchased. These examples explain why a death certificate answers who died but does not, by itself, identify every successor or signing authority.

## Selling through probate

A personal representative may sell estate property under the authority granted by the court and applicable law. Some sales require court confirmation; others may proceed under the Independent Administration of Estates Act with full authority and required notices, subject to objections and restrictions. Limited authority does not confer every power available under full authority.

In a confirmation sale, the accepted offer can be subject to a hearing and qualifying overbids. The initial buyer should understand that acceptance by the representative is not necessarily the final transfer. The process protects the estate's interests, and the court evaluates statutory requirements rather than merely honoring whichever buyer arrived first.

For an ordinary private real property sale requiring confirmation under Probate Code section 10309, both the appraisal and its valuation date must be within one year before the confirmation hearing, and the offered sum must be at least 90% of that appraised value. Statutory exceptions exist. Probate commissions are governed by the applicable contract and court authority; a salesperson should not assume the same payment procedure as an unrestricted private sale.

Under Probate Code section 10311, the statutory minimum first overbid generally adds 10% of the first $10,000 of the accepted bid and 5% of the amount above $10,000. The hearing can involve further bidding and procedural requirements. This formula does not determine every later increment or apply to every trust or independently administered sale.

### Independent administration is still administration

The Independent Administration of Estates Act can permit a personal representative with the required authority to act without obtaining a separate confirmation order for every qualifying sale. That does not eliminate fiduciary duties, notice requirements, restrictions in the appointment, or the effect of a proper objection. Full authority and limited authority must not be treated as identical.

A buyer considering an estate sale should determine whether court confirmation is required and whether the offer is subject to additional procedural conditions. A transaction advertised as "probate" might proceed differently from another probate transaction because the representative's authority differs. The label alone does not establish an overbid hearing or guarantee that no hearing will occur.

The representative must also distinguish estate expenses from personal expenses and handle proceeds for the estate. A brokerage should not disburse sale funds to a beneficiary merely because everyone expects that person eventually to inherit. Distribution authority, creditor claims, taxes, and the governing order or administration process may require a different destination and timing.

## Worked overbid

Suppose an accepted bid of $500,000 is presented for court confirmation and the statutory first-overbid formula applies. Ten percent of the first $10,000 is $1,000. Five percent of the remaining $490,000 is $24,500. Add both increments to $500,000 to obtain **$525,500**.

Do not add 10% to the entire $500,000. Do not subtract the first $10,000 from the final bid. The first segment and the remaining segment use different rates, and both increments are added to the accepted bid. Deposit, qualification, and hearing rules must also be met; the arithmetic alone does not qualify someone to bid.

### Appraisal threshold and overbid are separate calculations

Assume a private estate sale requires confirmation, the qualifying appraisal is $600,000, and no exception to the ordinary 90% rule applies. The threshold is $540,000. An accepted $550,000 offer clears that threshold. The first-overbid formula then uses the accepted $550,000 offer as its base, not the $600,000 appraisal.

Ten percent of the first $10,000 is $1,000. Five percent of the remaining $540,000 is $27,000. Add $28,000 to the accepted offer to obtain $578,000. The $540,000 figure has two different appearances here: it is the minimum appraisal-based price and coincidentally the portion of the accepted bid above $10,000. Those are different calculations, and different numbers would eliminate the coincidence.

If the accepted bid were only $520,000, computing an overbid would not by itself cure the original confirmation problem under the stated assumptions. First determine whether the sale satisfies applicable requirements. Then calculate the bidding threshold if the procedure calls for one. Price, bidder responsibility, payment terms, and judicial discretion remain separate considerations.

## Other involuntary and court-related transfers

**Foreclosure** enforces a security interest through the applicable judicial or nonjudicial process. A trustee's sale under a deed of trust and a judicial foreclosure are different procedures. Lien priority determines which interests may be extinguished and which may survive; buying at foreclosure does not guarantee a property free of every lien.

A **deed in lieu of foreclosure** is a voluntary conveyance to the lender as an alternative to completing foreclosure. Because it is not the foreclosure process itself, junior interests are not automatically eliminated. The lender examines title and the parties' settlement carefully.

A **tax sale** follows statutory enforcement of tax-defaulted property. A **sheriff's sale** can arise from execution of a judgment. A **partition** proceeding resolves cotenant interests through division or, when appropriate, sale and allocation of proceeds. **Condemnation** is the legal proceeding through which government exercises eminent domain, ordinarily requiring just compensation under the governing standards.

Bankruptcy can place property under federal court control and impose an automatic stay. A creditor or broker should not assume that ordinary enforcement or closing can continue unchanged after learning of a filing. Court authority, trustee powers, liens, and exemptions may determine the next steps.

### A special deed does not answer every title question

An investor buys at a foreclosure sale and later learns that a senior deed of trust remains. The phrase "foreclosure property" did not promise a debt-free asset. Determine the lien foreclosed, its priority, the procedure, and which interests survived. A deed in lieu raises a different issue because a voluntary conveyance does not perform the same extinguishment process.

Partition and condemnation also differ in purpose. Partition resolves competing ownership interests among cotenants through division or sale as appropriate. Condemnation implements a public acquisition under eminent-domain authority. Both can involve a court and a resulting conveyance, but neither should be classified as ordinary probate merely because a judge participates.

The exam method is to identify the event, the authority, and the resulting interest. Death, default, unpaid taxes, cotenant disagreement, and public acquisition trigger different rules. The name of the resulting deed helps identify the process, but the proceeding and valid authority determine what the buyer receives.

## Transfers through operation of law

**Adverse possession** can produce ownership after strict requirements are satisfied, including California's generally applicable five-year period, qualifying possession, and payment of assessed property taxes. It is different from a prescriptive easement, which concerns a use right rather than title. Public property is subject to protective rules that defeat ordinary adverse-possession assumptions.

**Accession** can alter ownership boundaries through events such as gradual accretion, subject to the governing water and boundary law. **Dedication** can create public rights through the owner's acts and required acceptance. These are not ordinary broker-negotiated conveyances, but they can affect what interest a seller actually has.

## Exam review

Identify the authority, proceeding, and interest transferred. A will does not itself avoid probate; a trust sale does not automatically require court confirmation; an expected heir cannot automatically sign for an estate. Apply the probate overbid formula only when the question makes it relevant, and never assume a special-purpose deed erases all prior rights.
